A confirmed new way for a Startup, a Referring Partner, or a Receiving Partner to start a connection with each other - in any direction, not just the two the SOP recognized before this - with Sarthy admin gating every step. All open design questions have been resolved directly with Sarthy leadership, and this model is now folded into Partnership SOP v1.0 Section 7, the Low-Level Spec's Lead state machine, the Data Dictionary's Lead table, and the BRD. This page stays as the detailed walkthrough and the record of how each design decision was reached.

Why this exists

Today's SOP only recognizes two ways a startup and a receiving partner end up connected: a referring partner picks the receiving partner directly (Path A), or the startup picks one itself from a list (Path B). Both of those are one person choosing, and one person waiting. This proposal adds a third direction - a receiving partner browsing startups and proposing to work with one directly - and puts the same admin-gated approval process in front of all three, so every connection, regardless of who started it, goes through the same consent-and-sanity-check step before anyone's contact details are shared.

In every row, "requesting" only ever means someone has expressed interest - it never means the connection exists yet. Every party involved can see that a request exists from the moment it's made, but only as a faded, FYI-only entry with nothing to act on - see below for what changes once admin has looked at it. Showing it at all, even in this inactive form, is deliberate: seeing a live request tends to nudge people to be more responsive and engaged on their own, before admin ever steps in - and it gives admin a shared reference point to open the sanity-check conversation with, instead of surprising the requested party cold. Being listed as a "requested party" means the same thing for whoever holds that role, startup included: real, required consent, not just a courtesy notice. That's confirmed explicitly for the referring-partner-initiated flow, where the startup is a requested party alongside the receiving partner - the first place in the SOP where a startup is formally asked to approve something, rather than only ever being the subject of someone else's decision.

How this would map onto today's Lead lifecycle

Confirmed: "admin approves" is now the equivalent of "Acknowledged" in the Low-Level Spec's Lead state machine - the 999-credit deduction fires there, exactly like today, just triggered by admin's decision instead of the receiving partner's own action. "Consent refused" is the equivalent of "Rejected" - no deduction, same as today. Everything downstream of that point - In Progress, Converted, the referral fee, disputes - stays exactly as already defined; this only changes how a Lead gets from nothing to Introduced-and-Acknowledged, not what happens after.

Resolved: the 4 open questions from the first draft

Answered directly with Sarthy leadership before this design was folded into the SOP, Low-Level Spec, and Data Dictionary:

  • Startup consent, formally - resolved: yes, real consent is required. In the referring-partner-initiated flow, the startup is a requested party in exactly the sense the receiving partner is - actual approval, not just being kept informed.
  • What the "sanity check" actually checks - resolved: three things. The requesting party's account is in good standing; the request isn't a duplicate of one already open between the same pair; and the requesting party is using the feature responsibly - not, for instance, working through the partner or startup list to harvest bulk contact details rather than genuinely pursuing a connection. That third check is a judgment call for admin, not something a system rule can fully automate on its own - worth watching once this is live, and worth admin having a simple way to flag a pattern of misuse.
  • How long a request can sit open - resolved: no automatic expiry. Unlike Lead validation (5 working days) or acknowledgment (7 days), an open connection request has no timeout - it stays open until admin acts, however long that takes. Flagging this plainly as an operational risk rather than glossing over it: with no SLA built into the rule itself, the size of the open-request backlog becomes purely a function of how fast admin works through the queue, with nothing in the system forcing a decision either way.
  • Multiple open requests on the same startup - resolved: yes, more than one can stay open at once. Since the same-service-type rule is a flag rather than a block at the request stage, two similar receiving partners can each have an open request to the same startup at the same time - the startup keeps its options rather than the system forcing an early choice.

One new question this resolution raises, not yet answered: when one of those open requests converts into an actual connection, do the startup's other open requests to similar-type partners close automatically, or stay open until someone acts on them too? The same-service-type guardrail was loosened to a flag at the request stage, but Partnership SOP v1.0 Section 7's underlying rule against serving a startup through two same-type partners at once presumably still applies once a connection is actually live, not just requested. Worth a direct answer before this reaches a developer as a build spec, since it decides what the system needs to do automatically the moment admin approves one of the open requests.

What changed as a result

With all 4 original open questions resolved, this is no longer a standalone proposal. Partnership SOP v1.0 Section 7 now describes this 3-flow model directly, replacing the old Path A/Path B-only description. The Low-Level Spec's Lead state machine now includes the request-visibility states (faded/FYI, then active) and treats admin approval as the trigger for the 999-credit deduction, generalized across all 3 directions. The Data Dictionary's Lead table carries the new fields this needs - request direction, visibility state, and consent status per party. The BRD reflects the 3-flow model in its requirements. This page remains in the hub as the detailed walkthrough and the record of why each design decision was made the way it was - the 4 documents above are now where the actual rules live day to day.
