This is the complete rulebook for how the partner program runs - the operational heart of Strategic Partners Playbook 3.0. It fully absorbs Sarthy's original Playbook 2.0: nothing from that document lives anywhere else anymore, and it should be treated as retired. Where a number or rule below is still genuinely unconfirmed, that's flagged clearly and linked to the open question - the rest is Sarthy's own design, carried forward in full.

1. What this document is for confirmed

One place that explains how partners join, how money and credits work, how we keep them engaged, how we group them, and what to do when something goes wrong. Building the marketplace app itself is not covered here - that's the developers' job, not ours.

2. Where this program came from, and why it changed confirmed

Sarthy spent over three years working closely with early-stage and growth-stage startups, and kept seeing the same problem: a startup's biggest obstacle usually isn't ambition, it's not knowing the right professional help at the right time. The partner program exists to close that gap, connecting startups with vetted providers across Marketing, Finance, Legal, Technology, and Strategy.

The first version of the program (2023-24) worked, but it only flowed in one direction: Sarthy referred startups to partners, and partners never referred business to each other. A marketing partner working closely with a startup is often the first to know that startup also needs a lawyer or a CFO - but the first version of the program had no way to reward that kind of cross-referral, so it never happened in practice. The redesign - what Sarthy called Playbook 2.0 - turns the partner network from a one-way list of vendors into a system where any partner can refer to, and receive from, any other partner, with Sarthy tracking it all and keeping things fair.

The idea in one line: in the old model, a partner was a vendor. In the current design, a partner is a node in a shared network where everyone has something to gain and something to manage.

3. Who does what confirmed

Lead handles the overall plan, writing things down, reporting to Sarthy, writing the technical requirements, and deciding how to handle problems. Associate makes calls, enters data, and does the first round of follow-up with partners. Sarthy decides the money rules, the app's roadmap, and how success is measured. Sarthy also stays involved as the programme's referee - every lead exchange runs through Sarthy's tracking, not directly between partners.

4. What partners actually get out of joining confirmed

  • Access to Sarthy's own base of early-stage and growth-stage startup clients
  • The right to send qualified leads to any other partner and earn credits when those leads convert
  • Incoming leads from other partners - a second source of new business beyond their own outreach
  • Sarthy's oversight - leads are tracked, conversions are confirmed, and credits are managed transparently, so partners aren't relying on informal trust alone
  • A chance to be featured as Partner of the Month, a recognition program running since April 2024 that showcases partner work to Sarthy's whole startup network
  • A listing in Sarthy's partner directory, with their case study and company profile visible to startups

The credit a partner earns isn't just a point score - it can be used to receive leads from other partners, or withdrawn once it's above the minimum reserve (see Section 10). A partner who consistently sends leads that convert builds up real value; a partner who only ever receives leads without referring any back will draw their balance down over time. The system is built to reward two-way participation, not one-sided use.

Paying the fee activates a partner's account and gives them their starting 999 credits immediately. Working assumption, pending Sarthy sign-off: both programmes grant 999 credits regardless of the fee difference. Currency only enters the picture once, at the moment a partner actually withdraws to real money (see Section 10) - the credit itself stays a neutral unit the whole time in between, including when it moves between an India-programme partner and an international one.

Joining steps: apply through LinkedIn, a mailer, the WhatsApp community, or a direct approach → Sarthy reviews the company profile and holds a discovery call with the founder or key contact → Sarthy evaluates fit (service relevance, affordability for early and growth-stage startups, how varied their client base is, professional standing, and whether they lean toward being a Giver or a Taker - see Section 11) → if both sides agree, the referral agreement is signed and uploaded to Sarthy's partner drive → the partner submits a case study and company profile → the partner pays the programme fee → their credit wallet activates with 999 opening credits → they're officially listed in the ecosystem.

6. The credit wallet drafted, one calculation basis still open

Every partner has a credit wallet that Sarthy maintains. It records every credit added or taken away as the partner participates.

The credit amount (999) is the same for every transaction, no matter the size of the deal or which service category it's in - this keeps the system simple and fair.

Working assumption, pending Sarthy sign-off: credits a partner paid real money for don't expire - they roll over indefinitely. Credits Sarthy hands out as a goodwill gesture or coupon expire 1 month (30 days) from the date they're received. There's no cap on how large a partner's balance can grow, consistent with letting paid credits roll over freely - worth revisiting only if hoarding becomes a real pattern (see Section 13's guardrails).

7. How a lead moves from one partner to another drafted, one mechanism still needs design

  1. A partner identifies a startup or contact that would benefit from another partner's services, and submits it to Sarthy.
  2. Sarthy checks the lead is genuine, fits the receiving partner's actual services, and isn't a duplicate - working assumption: Sarthy validates and passes a lead on within 5 working days, maximum.
  3. Sarthy introduces the lead to the receiving partner, along with context from whoever referred it.
  4. The receiving partner acknowledges the lead - at this point, 999 credits are deducted from their wallet, whether or not the lead eventually converts.
  5. The receiving partner works the lead.
  6. If it converts into a paid engagement, Sarthy confirms this and credits the referring partner - 999 credits, minus the 9% fee taken at the point of transfer (see Section 6). If it doesn't convert, the referring partner earns nothing - but the receiving partner's deduction stands either way.

All referrals have to go through Sarthy - two partners privately agreeing to swap leads outside the system doesn't count and won't be honoured at settlement. A valid lead is a real prospect with an actual near-term need, a real point of contact, and one that hasn't already been referred through the system or already engaged with that receiving partner before.

Working assumption, pending Sarthy sign-off:

  • Cancelling a submission: yes, a referring partner can cancel a lead before it reaches the receiving partner.
  • Duplicate leads: when a partner tries to submit a lead that's already in the system, the platform should catch it at the point of upload and flag it as invalid before it goes anywhere - not after. Alongside this, showing partners a list of existing leads would add transparency and cut down on accidental duplicates. Both of these are platform behaviors, not just policy - they need to be built, so they're carried forward into the eventual requirements document, not just written here as a rule.
  • Repeated declines: four or more leads declined in a row gets flagged to the admin, who steps in manually to understand what's actually going on and resolve it - not an automatic penalty, a prompt for a human to check.
  • Re-referring an unconverted lead: no fixed limit on how many times a lead can be re-referred - but never to two partners offering the same type of service at the same time, since that creates confusion and competing outreach to the same prospect. Referring the same lead to different types of service providers at once is fine, since the lead may genuinely need more than one kind of help.
  • Acknowledgment and rejection window: a receiving partner has 1 week to acknowledge a new lead. If they explicitly reject it within that week, the 999-credit deduction reverses in full. If the week passes with no resolution either way - no conversion, no explicit rejection - the deduction is final and won't be refunded.
  • At a stall past the 1-week window - proposed mechanism, open for discussion, not decided: the admin should still have discretion to manually reverse a deduction in genuinely disputed cases, even past the 1-week cutoff, based on the merit of the case. To make that judgment fairly, admin needs real evidence, not just the receiving partner's word. One workable approach: prompt the startup directly - a short check-in asking whether the partner actually reached out and engaged with them. That confirmation (or denial) becomes real evidence for the admin's decision. This still leaves open exactly how mandatory that startup confirmation should be, and what happens if the startup itself doesn't respond either. Flagged for further discussion, not resolved.
  • Who picks the receiving partner: this stays fully in the referring partner's hands, not Sarthy's - see Section 15 for how Sarthy's team supports and encourages this activity.
Refined, still not fully decided: the backend should include some form of matchmaking that acts as a catalyst - surfacing and promoting good potential matches to partners - rather than a separate anonymous pool that bypasses partner choice entirely. Partner selection stays the primary mechanism; the system's job is to actively nudge activity, similar to how Sarthy's own team is expected to touch base regularly and encourage sending and receiving leads (see above). This still needs real design work on what the backend actually surfaces and how, and isn't something to build into the current Alpha yet.

8. The six credit rules drafted, all six rules specified

  1. Opening balance: every partner starts with 999 credits on payment. This isn't refundable.
  2. One credit per lead: conversion is confirmed by Sarthy based on input from both partners - proof like a signed agreement, an invoice, or another clear confirmation that paid work has actually started.
  3. No double credits: one lead can only generate one deduction and one credit, ever - a deal expanding later doesn't create extra credits unless it's genuinely a new, separate referral.
  4. Credits can't be transferred between partners - they're only earned through real referrals and only cashed out at settlement. No partner can gift, sell, or hand off their credits to someone else.
  5. Fixed value: 999 credits per transaction, always, regardless of category or deal size.
  6. Negative balances - corrected: working assumption, pending Sarthy sign-off - a partner's balance can run negative down to -2,997 credits. Beyond that, their account freezes - they can't receive further leads until they've topped up back to +999 credits (their original entry balance, not just back to zero). A top-up costs 4 times the original entry fee (₹3,996 or $196) and adds 3,996 credits - which lands a partner frozen at the exact floor precisely at +999. The numbers now actually work in one payment, unlike the earlier version of this rule.

9. How conversion gets confirmed, and what happens if someone disagrees confirmed for now

Sarthy has the final say on whether a lead converted. Working assumption, pending Sarthy sign-off: the same conversion standard applies across every service category for now, rather than defining separate rules per category - first payment plus a digitally signed agreement completed through the platform. This means the platform needs real digital-signature capability, not just an upload of a scanned PDF - worth carrying into the eventual requirements document.

If a partner disagrees with Sarthy's conversion decision, they can raise it in writing within 14 days. Working assumption, pending Sarthy sign-off: the admin responds within 24 hours, engaging both parties directly to understand the concern - this can happen offline, over email, it doesn't need to be a formal in-platform process. Sarthy reviews the evidence and gives a final answer - Sarthy's decision on conversion disputes is final, not open to further appeal.

10. Withdrawing credits and staying a member drafted, exchange rate source still open

Working assumption, pending Sarthy sign-off: there is no fixed programme cycle. Membership is ongoing - a partner joins once and stays a member indefinitely, with no end date forcing a settlement. This is a real change from Sarthy's original Playbook 2.0 design, which was built around cycles ending in a settlement event; that cycle concept is intentionally dropped here in favor of continuous membership.

  • Minimum reserve: a partner must always keep at least their original entry amount (999 credits, whichever programme) sitting in their wallet.
  • Withdrawing: anything above that reserve can be withdrawn quarterly. Credits convert to real currency at whatever the live exchange rate is on the day of withdrawal, not the rate from whenever the partner originally joined - so the same credit balance can be worth slightly more or less in cash depending on when someone chooses to cash out. Still open: which specific exchange rate source we use as the official reference (a bank's published rate, a live market rate, something Sarthy sets internally) - a small detail, but one that needs a specific answer so there's no ambiguity at the moment of payout.
  • Where the 9% fee applies - resolved: it's charged once, at the moment credit transfers to a referring partner on conversion (see Section 6) - not a second time when they later withdraw their accumulated balance to real money. It's already taken at the source, so there's nothing left to deduct at withdrawal itself.
  • Pausing or stepping away: since there's no cycle to leave mid-way through, there's no forfeiture and no prorated payout to work out. A partner who goes quiet simply has their account and balance sit as they are - they can pick the relationship back up any time.

No partner should be told their credit balance is guaranteed cash before an actual withdrawal is processed.

11. How we group partners - Giver vs. Taker drafted

This framing goes back to the very first version of the program: some partners actively contribute to the ecosystem (Givers), others mostly draw from it without reciprocating (Takers). In the current design, this shows up directly in the credit wallet - a partner whose balance keeps growing is behaving like a Giver; a partner whose balance keeps shrinking is behaving like a Taker. Sarthy uses this trend as one input when reviewing partners periodically, and may deprioritize or exit partners who consistently show Taker behavior.

The same judgment gets applied earlier too, during onboarding: does the partner have a healthy, varied client base, are they established in their field, and do they seem collaborative rather than purely extractive. This isn't a hard checklist - it's a conversation-based read, made during the discovery call.

See the full Partner Segmentation Framework for the priority tiers this feeds into, and how it drives who the Associate reaches out to first.

12. What we expect from partners confirmed

  • Only submit real, qualified leads - not padding or guesses
  • Actually pursue leads they accept, in good faith, once the credit deduction has happened
  • Behave professionally with leads, other partners, and the Sarthy team
  • Report conversions honestly - not overstating or hiding what's actually happened
  • Keep their profile, case study, and service details current in the partner directory
  • Respond to Sarthy's requests for updates or documentation in reasonable time

All formal lead exchanges go through Sarthy - partners privately agreeing on referral arrangements or promising each other credits outside the system isn't recognized and won't be honoured at settlement.

13. Guardrails against abuse confirmed

A system built on financial credits needs clear rules against manipulation. These are not allowed, under any circumstance:

  • Submitting fake or made-up leads to earn credits
  • Submitting leads for contacts who have no real interest in or need for the receiving partner's services
  • Two partners coordinating to exchange fake leads with each other just to earn credits
  • Claiming a conversion happened when no paid work has actually started
  • Accepting a lead with no intention of following up, purely to deny the referring partner their credit
  • Submitting a lead for a startup that's already a client of the receiving partner, or already referred to them elsewhere

Sarthy also periodically reviews referral patterns across the whole network - a partner with consistently low conversion rates, or one who seems to be coordinating suspiciously with just one or two other partners, gets flagged for review. This isn't automatically punitive, it's a health check on the system. In cases of serious fraud that cause real financial harm, Sarthy reserves the right to escalate to legal action.

19. How we measure success drafted, proposal awaiting Sarthy confirmation

We're using Sarthy's own scorecard: finding and signing up partners (20%), talking to and supporting startups (40%), matching the right partner to the right startup (30%), and research and reporting (10%). The last two categories weren't clearly defined in Sarthy's own records, so here's a proposed definition for each - grounded in what this SOP now actually measures, not guesswork. This needs Sarthy's confirmation before it's treated as final, the same as everything else marked as a working assumption in this document.

Match-Making (30%) - proposed measures:

  • How many submitted leads get matched to at least one relevant partner each month
  • Average time from a lead being submitted to it actually reaching a partner - measured against the 5-working-day target in Section 7
  • What share of submitted leads get validated and matched, versus rejected or left stalled
  • Whether leads are reaching a healthy spread of partners over time, not just the same one or two every time

Research & Reports (10%) - proposed measures:

  • A recurring report - monthly is a reasonable starting cadence - summarizing partner and lead activity trends
  • Specific, written recommendations coming out of that report, and whether they actually get acted on
  • Partner feedback gathered on a regular basis (via the Alpha Partner Interview Guide process) and rolled into the same reporting

20. After the deal closes - the referral fee drafted

Everything in Sections 5-10 covers the 999-point credit exchanged when a lead is shared. This section covers a separate, second reward that only kicks in once a shared lead actually turns into a real, signed deal - a genuinely different track, running in parallel, not a replacement for the credit system.

  1. The receiving partner closes the lead - they sign an agreement directly with the startup.
  2. The value and duration of that agreement gets shared with Sarthy and with the referring partner who originally sent the lead.
  3. The referring partner is entitled to 10% of the total deal value.
  4. This flows through Sarthy, not directly between the two partners. Sarthy takes a 9% cut of that 10% as its facilitation fee - the same 9% principle used everywhere else in this document, just applied to a different base. The referring partner nets the remaining 91% of the 10% - which works out to 9.1% of the total deal value actually landing with them, not the full 10%.
  5. This amount accumulates in the referring partner's account with Sarthy over the year and gets paid out once annually, at the end of the financial year (March 31st).
  6. This entitlement only lasts 1 year from the lead's first engagement with the receiving partner. After that year, the receiving partner and the startup are free to continue their relationship without any ongoing obligation back to the original referring partner.

Working assumption, pending Sarthy sign-off: this resolves one of the oldest open questions in this whole document - whether the 9% facilitation fee also applies to this older-style referral-fee flow, separate from lead-gen credits. It does, at the same 9% rate, just calculated on the 10% entitlement rather than on a flat 999-credit transfer.

21. Staying an active partner drafted

This is the actual content for what was previously an empty section on winning back inactive partners - it turns out Sarthy's own design already has a real rule here, not just a gap to fill later.

Working assumption, pending Sarthy sign-off: a partner who doesn't share any lead for 3 consecutive months is marked dormant. A dormant partner is removed from the public portal listing and stops receiving leads from others, until they resume actively sharing again. The underlying principle: no partner gets to be purely a taker, permanently receiving without ever contributing - this connects directly to the Giver/Taker framing in Section 11. There's an explicit intent to gamify this further as the platform matures (leaderboards, streaks, recognition for consistent Givers) - not designed yet, flagged as a real direction rather than a vague aspiration.

22. Frequently asked questions confirmed

Do I lose my starting 999 credits if none of my referrals convert? No - the opening credits aren't tied to referral performance. But if you receive leads, each one still deducts 999 credits from your balance regardless.

What if I refer a lead and it doesn't convert? You don't earn credits for it. The receiving partner's deduction still stands either way - they accepted the lead.

Can I decline a lead I don't think is relevant? Yes - before the deduction happens, you'll see the context for the lead. If it's outside your service area, you can decline and no credit gets deducted.

Can I refer more than one lead to the same partner? Yes - each referral is its own separate transaction with its own credit or deduction.

What if a lead converts months later? The credit is awarded whenever conversion is actually confirmed, regardless of how much time has passed.

23. Closing note confirmed

This program is built on the belief that the strongest partner networks run on mutual accountability, not just shared access. Every partner who joins becomes a real stakeholder in a shared system - not a passive vendor waiting for the phone to ring. The credit model is deliberately simple and the rules are deliberately transparent, so that a partner's balance is an honest reflection of what they've actually put into the ecosystem. This program works when every partner is both a generous contributor and a serious professional - that's the standard we're building toward.
