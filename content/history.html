This page holds the "why" and the "what it used to be" - the history behind decisions in the hub. The other 25 pages describe what's true today. This page is where that older context lives instead, so it doesn't get in the way of a reader just trying to find out the current rule.

Why Partnership SOP v1.0 absorbed Sarthy's Playbook 2.0

Sarthy had already written a detailed design document called Playbook 2.0. It covered years of their own thinking on the credit system, the referral flow, and the rules against abuse.

Early in this project, that document sat outside the hub as a separate reference. Partnership SOP v1.0 only pointed to it.

Later, this was judged to create a real risk: two documents describing the same rules, with no guarantee they'd stay in sync. So every real piece of Playbook 2.0 was rewritten into Partnership SOP v1.0, section by section. Playbook 2.0 is now retired. Nothing about the program lives there that isn't also in the SOP.

Why Sarthy's original Playbook 1.0 was never absorbed

Playbook 1.0 was Sarthy's very first version of the program, from 2023-24. It was much simpler than Playbook 2.0 - mostly just a flat 10% referral fee, no credit wallet. Almost everything worth knowing from it is already captured in the Current-State Assessment's history section, so it was never pulled into the SOP the way Playbook 2.0 was.

Why the Master Execution Sequence's item 11 covers four different things

Item 11 used to be four separate items on the list: a new onboarding process, an engagement plan, a reactivation plan, and an escalation SOP. They were merged into one item. All four were always meant to become sections inside Partnership SOP v1.0, not standalone documents - keeping them as four separate to-do items was creating duplicate work.

Why two documents in the hub aren't numbered on the Sequence page

The Glossary and the Action Item & Blocker Tracker don't have numbers on the Master Execution Sequence page. That's not a gap - they simply weren't part of the original plan. Both came out of later requests as the hub grew.

Why the Gantt view was retired

The Project Plan (Gantt View) duplicated the Roadmap's own phase timeline - the two disagreed on Phase 2/3/4 durations by about a week each, and reconciling them would have added a second source of truth for the same information rather than removing one. Its other real content, a "what's actually still ahead" list, already substantially duplicated the Action Item & Blocker Tracker. Cut in favor of keeping the Roadmap as the single timeline view - it now carries a status column (Done / In Progress / Not started) so nothing about real-world progress was lost.

The Migration Wave Plan was briefly retired the same way, then restored. At the time, its "Wave 2" gate (special-agreement partners) didn't reference the Graduation Criteria's own readiness gate, despite the dependency map implying they were linked - but the document itself held real wave-sequencing data needed for actual post-launch migration work, so removing it outright went too far. It's back, still marked as a deferred, post-build activity rather than something being actively maintained pre-build. The Lead has since resolved the mismatch itself: the two gates are now tied together - Wave 2 needs both its own conditions (legal review, fee-split math) and the Graduation Criteria's targets satisfied, not either one alone. See the Version History for the exact sequence of that decision, the reversal, and the later fix.

Why the Partner Journey Map was folded into the User Journey Map

The hub used to carry two journey documents that sounded similar but covered different ground: the Partner Journey Map followed one partner's own account lifecycle (Awareness through Dormancy), while the Combined Journey Map followed one referral across all three parties at once (Origination through Deal Closure). They weren't duplicates of each other - but keeping them as two separately-numbered documents was creating exactly the confusion their similar names suggested.

They're now one document, the User Journey Map, in four sections: the Referral Timeline (the old Combined Journey Map, essentially unchanged), a Referring Partner Lifecycle and a Receiving Partner Lifecycle (both built from the old Partner Journey Map's single lifecycle, deliberately duplicated by role at the Lead's request), and a new Startup Lifecycle, explicitly marked proposed and unvalidated since no hub document had ever defined one before.

Worth being honest about one trade-off here: the Referring and Receiving Partner Lifecycle sections describe mostly the same underlying mechanics - the same eight stages, the same account-status rules - presented twice under different role labels. A handful of stages (most clearly, whether "first activity" means submitting a lead or receiving one) do genuinely differ by role and are written that way; the rest are intentionally identical rather than artificially varied. This was a deliberate choice to organize the document by role for readability, not a claim that a referring partner's core lifecycle mechanics are actually different from a receiving partner's.

Why Findings 1 and 2 in the User Journey Map moved here

Section A of the User Journey Map lines up the startup, the referring partner, and the receiving partner against one shared timeline, specifically to catch things that don't show up looking at any single lane alone. That exercise surfaced three findings. Two of them - Findings 1 and 2 - have since been resolved. This is the record of what they were and how they closed, kept here so the Journey Map itself can stay focused on what's true today rather than carrying the backstory.

Finding 1 - a consent decision, once made, wasn't communicated to the referring partner. Once admin's consent conversation concluded, only the receiving partner (or, in directions A and C, the startup) was told which way it went. The referring partner - who'd actually submitted the request - got nothing beyond the faded, FYI-only entry they could already see. Resolved directly with the Lead: every party connected to a request now gets told the outcome, not just the party admin spoke with. This became part of a broader rule at the same time: any status update admin makes on a lead reflects to every one of its connected parties, and every such action on the portal sends an automatic email to those parties and to admin - now the confirmed rule in Partnership SOP v1.0 Section 7.

Finding 2 - no message existed for the moment a referral actually paid off. The Communication Template Library had a reminder before the old acknowledgment window closed, and an annual referral-fee payout notice many months later - nothing in between. The moment a referring partner's lead converted and credit landed in their wallet, arguably the most motivating event in the whole credit system, went unmarked. Resolved by the same general notification rule as Finding 1: confirming a conversion is itself an action admin takes, so it's covered too - the referring partner now gets an automatic email the instant the credit lands. A dedicated, warmer template for this specific moment is still worth drafting - tracked as a next step in the Engagement Strategy Framework.

Finding 3 - whether a startup's own repeat engagement gets tracked - isn't part of this move. It's only partly resolved: the underlying rule is confirmed, but no field exists yet to measure it. It stays live in the User Journey Map, and is now also logged in the Gap & Risk Register.

Why the partner network changed from a one-way referral model

The first version of the partner program (2023-24) only flowed one way. Sarthy referred startups to partners. Partners never referred business to each other.

The redesign turned this into a network where any partner can refer to, and receive from, any other partner - with Sarthy tracking it all.

What changedFirst versionCurrent design
StructureSarthy to partner onlyAny partner to any partner, with Sarthy tracking everything
Cost to joinFree₹999 (India) or $49 (international)
Credit walletNoneStarts with 999 credits, earned and spent through referrals
How partners earnOnly from their own client workCredits for every referral that converts, plus their own client work
Cost of receiving a leadNone999 credits deducted per lead received
AccountabilitySigned agreement onlySigned agreement plus a tracked credit wallet and conversion record

In one line: in the old model, a partner was a vendor. In the current design, a partner is a node in a shared network, where everyone has something to gain and something to manage.

Why membership has no fixed cycle

Sarthy's original design was built around cycles - a membership period that ended in a settlement event. This SOP made a deliberate change: membership is now ongoing, with no cycle and no forced end date. A partner joins once and stays a member indefinitely.

Why the Segmentation Framework changed from 5 tiers to 2 fields

The Segmentation Framework used to be a single 5-tier list. It reused words like "At-Risk" and "Dormant" - the same words already meaning something different in the Data Dictionary's account status field. A reader seeing "At-Risk" in a spreadsheet couldn't tell whether it meant the segmentation tier or the account status.

It was redesigned into two separate fields with no overlapping words: status (what the account can do) and giving_pattern (how the partner behaves, only meaningful when status is Active).
